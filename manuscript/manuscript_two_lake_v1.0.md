# Metric-specific reliability of Sentinel-2 lake time-series reconstruction under incomplete sampling: dense-reference evaluation and transfer without retuning

## Abstract

Sentinel-2 records of inland waters are temporally incomplete, and reconstruction reliability may depend on whether the target is pointwise accuracy, seasonal peak timing, trajectory shape or seasonal integral. We evaluated linear interpolation, TIMESAT default double logistic and TIMESAT smoothing spline in two experiments. In Lake Erken, dense daily chlorophyll fluorescence (CHLF) was sampled on Sentinel-2 usable dates, isolating same-variable temporal reconstruction from satellite retrieval error. In Lake Vombsjön, reconstruction choices frozen in Erken were transferred without retuning and evaluated by withholding observed Sentinel-2 Maximum Chlorophyll Index (MCI) acquisitions, a satellite proxy rather than absolute chlorophyll-a. Under the actual Erken mask, linear interpolation had the lowest equal-year normalized root-mean-square error (nRMSE) (0.203, versus 0.223 for the spline and 0.250 for the double logistic), whereas the double logistic had the lowest descriptive common-support integral error. Controlled missingness showed increasing pointwise error with longer gaps and greater retrospective hidden-gap activity, while gaps containing the reference global peak reduced timing reliability. In the locked Vombsjön ACOLITE transfer, nRMSE increased from isolated to four-acquisition withholding for linear interpolation (0.165–0.254), double logistic (0.170–0.271) and spline (0.182–0.333). Because the two experiments used different targets and normalizations, their nRMSE values are not comparable. Within Vombsjön, withheld-date trajectory agreement and timing relative to the observed MCI maximum produced different method orderings. No method was uniformly most reliable across evaluated properties. Reconstruction reliability should therefore be evaluated against the property of interest and the structure of missing observations, rather than inferred from curve completion or a single aggregate error metric.

## 1. Introduction

Satellite observations can extend inland-water monitoring beyond the spatial and temporal coverage of conventional sampling, but lake colour remains difficult to measure consistently. Inland waters are optically complex, often small relative to land-oriented pixels, and affected by atmospheric correction, land adjacency, changing mixtures of phytoplankton, suspended particles and coloured dissolved organic matter (Palmer et al., 2015a; Tyler et al., 2016). Sentinel-2 adds red and red-edge bands at spatial resolutions useful for many lakes and a nominal multi-satellite revisit interval of several days (Drusch et al., 2012). The usable temporal record is nevertheless much sparser than the acquisition calendar because clouds, shadows, cirrus, snow, ice and locally invalid water pixels remove observations.

Incomplete sampling matters because phytoplankton blooms are events, not only distributions of concentration. A reconstruction can therefore be judged by several properties that need not agree: pointwise error at unobserved dates, the timing and magnitude of the seasonal peak, agreement with the shape of the trajectory, and the seasonal integral as a measure of cumulative exposure. A curve can have a small average error while missing a short bloom, or reproduce a seasonal integral while shifting the dominant peak to another date. Previous work has demonstrated the use of TIMESAT and alternative smoothing approaches to retrieve phytoplankton phenology from satellite time series (Jönsson and Eklundh, 2004; Palmer et al., 2015b). The reliability of those metrics, however, depends on more than how many observations remain. It also depends on where gaps fall relative to the seasonal signal, on the dynamics concealed inside each gap, and on the shape constraints imposed by the reconstruction method. Two masks of similar observation density can pose very different problems if one removes a stable period and the other hides a rapid increase or the seasonal maximum.

Evaluation is especially difficult when satellite retrieval error and temporal reconstruction error are mixed. A sparse satellite-derived chlorophyll series differs from an in situ record because of radiometry, atmospheric correction, horizontal and vertical sampling, bio-optical conversion and timing. If reconstruction is evaluated only against occasional field samples, these sources of error cannot be separated. Lake Erken, Sweden, allows the temporal component to be examined on its own. Its continuous daily chlorophyll-fluorescence (CHLF) record (Erken Laboratory, 2026) can be sampled on the dates actually usable in the Sentinel-2 archive, reconstructed from those dates, and compared with the withheld daily CHLF values. Because the same variable is both reconstructed and evaluated, this design isolates the information lost through the real observation calendar and the temporal method from satellite retrieval error. It does not validate satellite retrievals, nor does it imply that daily fluorescence is equivalent to satellite surface chlorophyll. The link to satellite observations is therefore examined separately, using exact-date matchups between CHLF and two red-edge indices. These are the Maximum Chlorophyll Index (MCI; Gower et al., 2005) and the Normalized Difference Chlorophyll Index (NDCI; Mishra and Mishra, 2012), derived from top-of-atmosphere, official Level-2A bottom-of-atmosphere and ACOLITE aquatic atmospheric-correction products (Vanhellemont and Ruddick, 2018). Throughout, MCI is treated as a satellite proxy sensitive to chlorophyll, not as absolute Chl-a concentration. Expressing it as concentration requires explicit calibration and validation against field measurements (Salls et al., 2024).

A single-lake evaluation cannot establish whether reconstruction patterns persist under a different observation calendar and target variable. We therefore transferred the frozen reconstruction framework from Erken, without Vombsjön retuning, to Vombsjön, a second Swedish lake. The transferred settings were frozen from Erken evidence before Vombsjön transfer performance was inspected, and neither Vombsjön performance nor Vombsjön field data were used to choose them. Vombsjön has no equivalent dense daily ecological reference. Its primary quantitative evaluation therefore withholds observed ACOLITE MCI acquisitions, either singly or in consecutive blocks, and compares the reconstructions with the withheld observations. This target retains satellite observation uncertainty and evaluates reconstruction in observed-proxy units rather than against daily ecological truth. ACOLITE's primary status followed its pre-specified aquatic atmospheric-correction role, not a ranking of processor performance. Official L2A and L1C series were analysed separately, each on its own unmatched observation calendar, as sensitivity and diagnostic layers. Sparse exact-date field Chl-a measurements provide complementary proxy-consistency evidence only. The two lakes differ in reconstructed quantity, evaluation reference and error normalization. The transfer therefore tests whether patterns of reconstruction reliability persist, not whether numerical skill levels coincide. A second lake extends the conditions under which the methods are evaluated, but it does not establish universal validation.

We compared three reconstruction methods: linear interpolation as an untuned baseline, TIMESAT double logistic with default settings as a fixed benchmark, and a TIMESAT smoothing spline with its smoothing parameter selected in Erken. First, we evaluated how these methods preserve distinct reconstruction properties under the actual Sentinel-2 observation mask in Erken: pointwise error, global-peak timing, peak magnitude, trajectory agreement and seasonal integral. Second, we examined how controlled additional missingness conditions Erken reconstruction reliability. These experiments used random deletion and consecutive deletion windows that differed in duration, in hidden-gap activity characterized retrospectively from the withheld reference, and in whether they contained the reference peak. Third, we tested the previously frozen choices in a locked Vombsjön transfer against withheld observed ACOLITE MCI, under isolated and consecutive observed-acquisition withholding, and examined the annual heterogeneity of that performance. Finally, we used predefined sensitivity, diagnostic and observed-proxy evidence to establish the scope and limits of interpretation. This evidence comprised an alternative double-logistic seasonal parameter, peak-timing tolerances, separate L2A and L1C layers, the Erken satellite-index–CHLF matchups and the Vombsjön field pairs. A supplementary multiple-event analysis, specified after the global-peak results had been inspected, is reported as exploratory and does not replace the predefined global-peak evaluation.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1 Study design and evidence streams

The study combined two lakes with different observational roles (Fig. 1; Table 1). Lake Erken provided a dense daily chlorophyll-fluorescence (CHLF) record for examining temporal reconstruction under actual Sentinel-2 sampling. Lake Vombsjön provided an independent Sentinel-2 series for testing reconstruction choices that had been frozen in Erken. Four evidence streams were kept separate throughout.

1. **Erken dense-reference temporal reconstruction.** CHLF was sampled on usable Sentinel-2 dates, reconstructed from those dates, and evaluated against the withheld daily CHLF. The same variable was reconstructed and evaluated, so this stream isolates temporal sampling and reconstruction error from satellite retrieval error. It does not test retrieval accuracy.
2. **Erken satellite-index–CHLF observation-layer comparison.** Sentinel-2 red-edge indices from three processing levels were paired with CHLF on exact calendar dates. This stream examines whether observed indices carry chlorophyll-related information. It involves no reconstruction.
3. **Locked Vombsjön withheld-MCI transfer.** The frozen methods were applied to Vombsjön Maximum Chlorophyll Index (MCI) observations without retuning. Each reconstruction was evaluated against observed acquisitions withheld from it. This stream evaluates reconstruction in observed-proxy units and retains satellite observation uncertainty.
4. **Vombsjön sparse field proxy-consistency comparison.** Field chlorophyll-a (Chl-a) measurements were paired with observed MCI on exact calendar dates. They provide complementary proxy-consistency evidence only. They did not enter fitting or tuning and do not provide a daily reconstruction reference.

The evidence hierarchy was specified with the frozen designs. Primary quantitative evidence comprised Erken actual-mask reconstruction against withheld CHLF and Vombsjön ACOLITE reconstruction against withheld observed MCI. Secondary quantitative evidence comprised two analyses:
- Erken controlled-missingness experiments;
- Vombsjön observed-proxy peak timing, which could be evaluated only where the observed peak was identifiable.

Sensitivity analyses comprised official Level-2A (L2A) processing, a cross-validated double-logistic seasonal parameter, and alternative peak-timing tolerances. Level-1C (L1C) top-of-atmosphere processing served as a diagnostic baseline. The Vombsjön field pairs provided complementary descriptive proxy-consistency evidence, while the supplementary Erken seasonal-event analysis was treated as secondary/exploratory. The Erken observation-layer comparison provided separate lake-specific observed-proxy evidence and did not constitute a reconstruction test.

The two lakes differed in four respects:
- the reconstructed quantity (CHLF versus MCI);
- the evaluation reference (withheld dense daily values versus withheld observed acquisitions);
- error normalization;
- the unit of additional missingness (calendar-day windows versus blocks of observed acquisitions).

Numerical skill values were therefore not compared between lakes. The cross-lake comparison concerns whether metric-specific patterns of reliability persisted.

### 2.2 Lake Erken reference and Sentinel-2 observation mask

Lake Erken is a monitored Swedish lake with a Sentinel-2 reference coordinate at 59.84029° N, 18.625827° E. The reference dataset was supplied by the Swedish Infrastructure for Ecosystem Science (Erken Laboratory, 2026). It contains daily CHLF adjusted using laboratory chlorophyll measurements. The record spans 17 April 2019 to 30 November 2025 and contains 2,420 unique calendar dates. Within that interval there are no missing or duplicate dates and no non-finite or negative values. CHLF is reported in µg L⁻¹.

The provider's daily value is the 00:00 observation from higher-frequency sonde measurements, selected to reduce non-photochemical quenching. Measurement configurations changed during the record:
- 2019–2022: a YSI profiler from spring to autumn and a YSI sonde under ice;
- 2023–2025: the metadata include measurements from the Malma Island pumping system at approximately 3 m depth.

Original values were retained together with a broad pre-2023 versus 2023-onward provenance label, which was not interpreted as a causal instrument change. The series is a dense pelagic chlorophyll temporal reference. It carries its own measurement, depth and spatial-support uncertainty and is not equivalent to satellite surface chlorophyll.

The source records ice presence, and 1,950 dates were classified as open water. Ice-covered dates remained part of the reference record but lay outside the satellite-observable evaluation domain. The record begins and ends during open water. Therefore 2019 and 2025 were treated as boundary-truncated years and evaluated only over their observed common support. Their common-support maxima were not interpreted as full-calendar-year maxima.

The local archive contained 950 Sentinel-2 Level-2A products on 926 unique dates within the reference interval (Drusch et al., 2012). Scene quality was assessed at the station coordinate from each product's native Scene Classification Layer (SCL). SCL was used only to define observation availability; it did not select a reflectance product. A centred 3 × 3 window on the 20 m grid (60 × 60 m) was frozen as the primary neighbourhood.

A product passed when its 3 × 3 window met all of the following conditions:
- at least eight water pixels;
- no more than one pixel in an obviously invalid class;
- no persistent non-water or class-2 pixel;
- a centre pixel that was not in an obviously invalid class.

The rule was selected from the SCL observation process alone, without reference to CHLF, spectral-index values or reconstruction results. The calendar date was the temporal observation unit. A date was usable when at least one same-day product passed, and it contributed one observation. The rule retained 307 unique usable dates. Intersecting these dates with open water and finite CHLF produced 288 actual-mask sparse inputs across seven years. All reconstruction methods received identical date–value pairs. Alternative window sizes and the full SCL class logic are reported in the Supplementary Information.

For each year, common support comprised open-water dates from the first through the last sparse input. Disconnected open-water segments within that interval were kept separate. No method received credit for extrapolation outside these limits, and later controlled-missingness experiments did not move them.

### 2.3 Reconstruction methods and Erken evaluation

The benchmark contained three reconstruction methods.

- **Linear interpolation** was a fixed, untuned baseline between retained dates, without extrapolation.
- **TIMESAT double logistic** used the effective defaults of TIMESAT 4.4.1 accessed through timesat-cli 1.9.2 (Jönsson and Eklundh, 2004), including the default seasonal parameter `p_seapar = 1`. The full effective-default configuration was captured as an immutable snapshot before any performance output was generated. A runtime mismatch with that snapshot caused failure rather than silent adoption.
- **TIMESAT smoothing spline** used the fixed candidate grid {0, 1, 3, 10, 30, 100, 300, 1000}.

The spline smoothing parameter was selected by nested leave-one-year-out evaluation. For each outer test year, each candidate reconstructed each of the other six years separately from that year's sparse inputs. Candidates were scored by the equal-year mean of the six year-level normalized root-mean-square errors (nRMSE). The held-out test year's dense reference was excluded from its spline-parameter selection; dense CHLF from the training years entered only this predefined candidate-scoring procedure, and seasonal metrics did not enter tuning. A candidate that failed in any training year was ineligible, and exact ties favoured the smaller smoothing value. The selected values for 2019–2025 were 10, 100, 100, 10, 10, 3 and 10, respectively.

Each reconstruction received only its sparse input dates and values. Method failures, non-convergence and unavailable metrics were retained and reported rather than removed from denominators. Negative reconstructed values were retained without clipping.

Erken evaluation used only common-support dates that were open water, had a finite reference value, and had not been supplied as sparse inputs. Pointwise metrics were bias, mean absolute error (MAE), root-mean-square error (RMSE) and nRMSE, with errors defined as reconstruction minus reference. Erken nRMSE was normalized by the dense reference itself:

`nRMSE_y = RMSE_y / (Q95_y − Q05_y)`

Here Q95_y and Q05_y are the 95th and 5th percentiles of the daily CHLF reference over the common support of year y. When this scale was zero or non-finite, nRMSE was unavailable, and no stabilizing constant was added. Outcomes were first reduced to one value per method and year. Equal-year summaries then averaged the available annual values, so dense daily values were not treated as independent seasonal replicates.

The primary timing target was the global maximum within each year's common support. Its definition was applied identically to the reference and to each reconstruction:
- a contiguous equal-height plateau was assigned its temporal midpoint;
- non-contiguous equal global maxima made peak timing unavailable;
- a reference maximum on a support boundary was flagged and not treated as an ordinarily identifiable peak.

Signed and absolute peak-date errors were recorded. The primary tolerance criterion for timing success was an absolute error of at most 10 calendar days, with 5- and 15-day tolerances as sensitivities. A failed or unavailable reconstructed peak in a reference-eligible year counted as a non-success.

Three further measures were recorded:
- **Peak magnitude:** signed and absolute error, plus normalized absolute peak-magnitude error on the same Q95 − Q05 scale.
- **Common-support integral:** daily trapezoidal integration within each contiguous open-water segment, summed across segments without bridging non-open-water intervals.
- **Trajectory agreement:** Pearson correlation between reconstructed and reference daily trajectories over identical eligible common-support dates, used as a supporting measure alongside magnitude-sensitive error.

Uncertainty in Erken equal-year summaries was represented by 10,000 percentile bootstrap resamples of whole years. The same sampled years were used for all methods within a comparison, so method pairing was preserved.

### 2.4 Controlled missingness and Erken observation-layer comparison

Controlled missingness experiments started from each year's actual-mask sparse inputs. They added missingness to the observed sampling pattern rather than replacing it with an idealized revisit sequence. The first and last sparse inputs of each year were protected, so artificial deletion did not alter common-support boundaries. All methods received identical artificial masks.

**Random deletion.** Deterministic random deletion removed 10%, 20%, 30% or 50% of each year's interior sparse inputs. There were 100 replicates per year and deletion level, giving 2,800 masks.

**Consecutive windows.** Exhaustive consecutive-gap experiments slid 10-, 20-, 30- and 45-day calendar windows through each year's common support. A window was retained when it met all of the following conditions:
- it lay entirely within one contiguous open-water segment;
- it removed no support endpoint;
- it removed at least one sparse input.

This produced 5,746 windows. Each window recorded its duration, the number of sparse inputs removed, its relative position within the segment, and whether it contained the reference global peak.

Hidden-gap activity was summarized for consecutive windows as

`A_gap = Σ_(t=a+1,...,b) |C_t − C_(t−1)| / (Q95_y − Q05_y)`

for a window [a, b], where C_t is the daily reference. The sum includes only day-to-day transitions lying wholly inside the window. A_gap was computed from the dense reference that had been deliberately hidden from the reconstruction. It is therefore a retrospective, descriptive characterization of the concealed dynamics. It is not available inside a real, unknown observation gap and is not an operational predictor. A_gap was analysed as a continuous variable; its association with reconstruction error was summarized, for each method and window duration, by the within-year Spearman correlation between A_gap and scenario nRMSE, averaged with equal year weight. Low, medium and high activity classes, defined by tertiles within each window duration, were used only for display.

Random-deletion masks were characterized by deletion level rather than A_gap. Scenario outcomes were first averaged within each year and stratum (deletion level, window duration, activity class or peak containment) before equal-year summaries were formed; peak-timing success used reference-eligible scenarios as its denominator, with a failed or unavailable reconstructed peak counted as a non-success. No method was retuned by scenario: each year's spline setting was inherited from its outer-fold actual-mask selection, and the other two methods remained fixed. Deterministic seeding and other implementation details are given in the Supplementary Information.

The Erken observation-layer comparison was conducted separately, because the reconstruction experiment sampled CHLF itself and involved no satellite retrieval. It used three reflectance products:
- Level-1C top-of-atmosphere reflectance;
- official Level-2A bottom-of-atmosphere reflectance;
- ACOLITE surface reflectance (`rhos`; Vanhellemont and Ruddick, 2018).

Bands B4, B5 and B6 were extracted on a common 20 m grid, and no empirical cross-baseline correction was applied. The Normalized Difference Chlorophyll Index (NDCI) was computed as (B5 − B4)/(B5 + B4) (Mishra and Mishra, 2012). MCI was computed as B5 minus a baseline interpolated between B4 and B6 at 705 nm, using nominal wavelengths of 665, 705 and 740 nm (Gower et al., 2005). Both indices were calculated at pixel level. An observation required at least six valid pixels in the 3 × 3 window, and its value was the median of the valid pixel-level indices.

The calendar date was the only CHLF matchup key. Nearest-date substitution and temporal interpolation were not permitted. For each index, the primary comparison used processor-shared support, meaning dates that met all of the following conditions:
- exact L1C–L2A–ACOLITE source alignment;
- open water;
- finite same-day CHLF;
- eligibility for that index in all three products.

The three products were therefore compared against identical CHLF dates. The primary association measure was the Spearman correlation between each index and raw CHLF. Uncertainty was estimated from 10,000 percentile bootstrap resamples of whole calendar years. Products were compared descriptively, and no processor winner was declared. This comparison assessed the relevance of the observed indices to chlorophyll variability. It did not define an absolute Chl-a calibration and did not assume spatial or depth equivalence between satellite and in situ observations. Radiometric offset handling and a secondary leave-one-year-out regression are reported in the Supplementary Information.

### 2.5 Locked transfer from Erken to Vombsjön

The reconstruction choices carried to Vombsjön were fixed, using Erken evidence, in a dated, machine-readable transfer freeze before Vombsjön reconstruction performance was inspected. Vombsjön performance and field observations were not used for reconstruction tuning, and the transfer execution performed no retuning. A later pre-performance amendment (v1.1) changed only the spatial support of the field comparison (Section 2.7); it altered no reconstruction setting.

The three primary methods were transferred unchanged in role:
- **Linear interpolation** remained the untuned baseline.
- **Default TIMESAT double logistic** retained `p_seapar = 1` and the frozen effective-default snapshot.
- **TIMESAT smoothing spline** used `p_smooth = 10`. This value was obtained by applying the original candidate grid, scoring rule, candidate-failure rule and smaller-value tie-break to all seven Erken years with equal year weight. It coincides with the most frequent outer-fold selection but was not chosen on that basis. Because it uses all Erken years, this final setting is not itself an independent Erken validation.

All seven training-only outer folds of the separately reported double-logistic sensitivity selected `p_seapar = 0`. This cross-validated setting was carried forward as a sensitivity only and did not replace the default benchmark.

Several further elements were inherited from Erken:
- **Primary proxy.** MCI was designated the primary observed proxy on the basis of the Erken observation-layer comparison. It was treated throughout as a satellite proxy rather than absolute Chl-a.
- **Observation rules.** The six-of-nine valid-pixel rule and the calendar date as the observation unit were carried forward.
- **Fitting constraints.** Separate fitting by calendar year, no extrapolation beyond support, retention of failures and no clipping of negative values were carried forward.
- **Peak rules.** The global-peak plateau, ambiguity and boundary rules, the ±10-day primary tolerance criterion and the ±5- and ±15-day tolerance sensitivities were carried forward where applicable.

Elements specific to the Vombsjön satellite series were frozen at the same stage and are described in Section 2.6. These comprise the fixed station target, training-only scaling, the withholding design and the observed-proxy metrics.

ACOLITE `rhos` was designated the primary processing product according to the study's pre-specified role for a primary aquatic atmospheric correction. The designation did not rest on evidence that ACOLITE outperformed the other products: Erken MCI association intervals overlapped across processors, and no processor winner was declared. Official L2A and L1C MCI were retained as a separate sensitivity and a diagnostic baseline, respectively. Each was evaluated on its own observation calendar, without pooling, fallback between products or cross-calibration.

The freezes were staged and should be read as such:
- The primary Erken reconstruction contract was frozen before the first Erken performance comparison.
- The supplementary seasonal-event protocol was frozen after the global-maximum results had been inspected. It is therefore secondary and exploratory.
- The double-logistic seasonal-parameter sensitivity was specified after the primary Erken results had been inspected but before its own results were generated. It is a secondary sensitivity.
- The transfer freeze preceded inspection of Vombsjön reconstruction performance.

### 2.6 Vombsjön satellite MCI series and withholding experiment

Lake Vombsjön is a eutrophic lake in southern Sweden with a surface area of 11.8 km², a mean depth of 6.6 m and a maximum depth of 16 m (Rabow et al., 2025). The Vombsjön transfer used Sentinel-2 MCI from three processing levels, each treated as a separate series. ACOLITE surface reflectance (`rhos`; Vanhellemont and Ruddick, 2018) formed the primary series according to the frozen study design. Official L2A bottom-of-atmosphere reflectance was a separate processing sensitivity, and L1C top-of-atmosphere reflectance was a diagnostic baseline. Each processor kept its own observation calendar, so the three calendars are independent and unmatched. Series were not pooled, missing products were not replaced by another processor, and no cross-calibration between processing levels was applied.

The temporal target was a fixed 3 × 3 window on the 20 m grid centred on the nominal station at 55.6775° N, 13.60889° E. The window did not move between dates. MCI was calculated at pixel level as

`MCI = B5 − [B4 + ((705 − 665)/(740 − 665)) × (B6 − B4)]`

using the nominal B4, B5 and B6 wavelengths of 665, 705 and 740 nm (Gower et al., 2005). An observation required valid B4, B5 and B6 in at least six of the nine pixels, and its value was the median of the valid pixel-level MCI values. When more than one eligible product of the same processor was available on a calendar date, the observation-level medians were reduced to their median. Each date therefore contributed one observation.

Pixel validity followed each product's native quality assessment. The official SAFE products used their band-quality and cloud/snow classification masks together with a water-class requirement from the paired L2A SCL. ACOLITE used its own flag layer. An observation was unavailable when a required quality family was missing, and invalid pixels were not filled. Negative reflectance and negative MCI values were retained without clipping.

The analysis season was the calendar year. Support extended from the first to the last quality-controlled observation of each year. There was no cross-year fitting and no extrapolation beyond the retained training dates. Vombsjön support was defined by observation availability, not by an independently mapped open-water period. Observed support in 2026 was partial and ended on 3 August for ACOLITE, 20 September for L2A and 11 August for L1C. All support boundaries were retained as observed rather than aligned across processors.

Each calendar year was fitted separately. Day 366 was to be excluded under the frozen TIMESAT setting; no eligible observation fell on day 366, so no date was removed. The frozen TIMESAT configuration expects inputs within a fixed absolute range, whereas MCI is small and signed. For both TIMESAT methods, each year and withholding scenario therefore applied a positive affine transformation fitted to the retained training observations only, mapping their minimum to 1000 and their maximum to 9000. Withheld values never entered this transformation. Predictions were transformed back to native MCI units before any evaluation. Scenarios with a constant or non-finite training range were unavailable for all methods.

Withholding scenarios were enumerated exhaustively within each processor and year. A year was eligible when it contained at least eight dates with valid observations, and every scenario had to retain at least six training observations. The first and last observations of each year were always retained. Two designs were used:
- **Isolated withholding:** each internal observation was withheld once.
- **Consecutive withholding:** every internal sequence of two, three or four consecutive observed acquisitions was withheld.

These blocks are defined by the number of observed acquisitions. Their calendar duration varies with the observation calendar. Within a processor, all methods received identical training and withheld dates. Settings were never adjusted by scenario.

Vombsjön evaluation was defined in observed MCI units and differs from the Erken dense-reference evaluation. Evaluation dates were the withheld dates at which the observation and the predictions of all three primary methods were finite. If any primary method failed, that scenario's paired primary metrics were unavailable. Errors were defined as prediction minus observation. Native-MCI bias, MAE and RMSE were computed for each scenario. nRMSE divided the scenario RMSE by the Q95 − Q05 range of that scenario's retained training observations, with no stabilizing constant. An invalid scale made only nRMSE unavailable.

Scenario metrics were averaged within each processor, year, design, block size and method. Annual RMSE was therefore the mean of scenario RMSEs. Equal-year estimates averaged the available annual values. Because the Vombsjön scale is derived from sparse retained observations rather than a dense withheld reference, its nRMSE is not numerically comparable with Erken nRMSE.

Trajectory correlation was evaluated at withheld dates. A date could be withheld in several overlapping scenarios, so repeated predictions were first collapsed to their median within each year, design, block size, method and date. Pearson correlation between these collapsed predictions and the observed MCI was calculated for each year when at least three dates were available and both series varied. Annual correlations were averaged arithmetically, without a Fisher transformation. These correlations describe agreement at withheld observation dates, not agreement with a dense daily trajectory.

Conditional observed-proxy peak timing was evaluated as a secondary, conditionally identifiable metric within each processor series. The reference was the global maximum among quality-controlled observed MCI values within the annual support, assigned under the frozen plateau, ambiguity and boundary rules. A reference maximum at a support boundary was not identifiable. A scenario was eligible for peak evaluation only when it withheld the date of that observed maximum. The reconstructed daily global maximum was located over the same support, and signed and absolute calendar-day errors were recorded. Continuous peak-error summaries required identifiable peaks from all three primary methods.

Within this metric, the primary tolerance criterion for success was an absolute error of at most 10 days, with 5- and 15-day tolerances as sensitivities. All eligible scenarios formed the success denominator. A fitting failure or an unavailable reconstructed peak counted as a non-success. This measure describes timing relative to the observed satellite-proxy maximum; it does not describe timing of an unobserved ecological bloom peak. No seasonal integral was evaluated for Vombsjön, because no dense reference exists against which an integral could be assessed.

### 2.7 Vombsjön field proxy-consistency comparison

The Vombsjön field record, obtained from the Dryad dataset associated with Rabow et al. (2025) (https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.02v6wwq7s), comprised 54 sampling dates: 6 in 2018, 22 in 2019 and 26 in 2020. Chl-a was measured by laboratory fluorometry on water-column-integrated samples (Rabow et al., 2025). The integration interval was 0–2 m in 2018 and 0–6 m in 2019–2020 (Rabow et al., 2025). Measured GPS coordinates were available on 23 dates. Of these, 21 passed coordinate quality control. Two dates, 10 June and 24 June 2020, carry an unresolved longitude discrepancy in the source data and were retained without correction. The remaining 31 dates have no measured coordinate. No field sampling clock times were available.

Satellite values for the field comparison came from one fixed pelagic sampling area, not from the nominal-station window used for the temporal series. The area was the unbuffered convex hull of the 21 quality-controlled GPS coordinates plus the nominal station as an anchor. It was identical on every field date and did not depend on any date's own coordinate. Dates without accepted GPS, including the two unresolved dates, contributed no vertex but remained eligible for comparison. No nominal-point fallback was used.

Pixels whose centres fell inside the area on the common 20 m grid were summarized by the median of their valid pixel-level MCI. An observation required at least two-thirds of the area's pixels to be valid, and native quality rules were applied as for the temporal series. Same-day products were reduced as in Section 2.6. The fixed area and its validity rule were adopted in a pre-performance amendment to the field-comparison support. That amendment left the temporal target and all reconstruction settings unchanged.

Field and satellite observations were paired on exact calendar dates, with zero temporal tolerance and no nearest-date substitution. Valid pairs numbered 7 for ACOLITE (four in 2019 and three in 2020), 9 for L2A and 9 for L1C. No processor had a valid pair in 2018. Field values were loaded only after the temporal reconstruction evaluation was complete and never entered fitting, tuning or method selection.

The paired records were examined descriptively as complementary evidence of proxy consistency. No calibration, regression or correlation was calculated. Integrated-water-column samples were not assumed equivalent to satellite-surface Chl-a. Exact-date pairing does not resolve the remaining differences in horizontal support, vertical support and sampling time. Polygon geometry, pixel counts, reasons for unavailable pairs and a secondary actual-GPS 3 × 3 sensitivity are reported in the Supplementary Information.

### 2.8 Sensitivity and uncertainty analyses

The cross-validated double logistic was a secondary sensitivity to the default seasonal parameter. In Erken, `p_seapar` was selected for each outer test year from the grid 0.0–1.0 in steps of 0.1. Selection used only the remaining training years and the same equal-year nRMSE criterion as the spline; exact ties favoured the larger value. The selected value was applied unchanged to the held-out year and to its controlled-missingness scenarios. All seven folds selected `p_seapar = 0`, which was carried to Vombsjön as a separately reported sensitivity. It was evaluated on the primary common dates only where its own fit succeeded and never altered primary denominators.

For peak-timing analyses in both lakes, ±10 days was the primary tolerance criterion, with ±5 and ±15 days as sensitivities. In Vombsjön, official L2A and L1C were evaluated with the same withholding designs and estimators as ACOLITE, but each on its own calendar and support. Differences between processors therefore combine processing with observation availability, so the design does not support a processor ranking.

The calendar year was the unit of replication in both lakes. Daily values and overlapping scenarios were treated as nested observations, not independent seasons. Erken equal-year uncertainty used 10,000 percentile bootstrap resamples of whole years. The same sampled years were used for all methods within a comparison, giving paired method contrasts. Erken paired year-level differences were used to qualify method contrasts (Section 3.2), and leave-one-year-out re-summaries describing their stability are reported in the Supplementary Information.

Vombsjön uncertainty also used 10,000 whole-year percentile bootstrap resamples, drawn separately for each processor, withholding design and block size. Within each such stratum, the same sampled years were reused across methods and metrics, and unavailable annual estimates were carried through each draw. Intervals were not reported for strata with fewer than two available years. No paired inference across processors and no Vombsjön leave-one-year-out analysis were performed.

A supplementary Erken analysis of multiple seasonal events was specified after the global-maximum results had been inspected. It is reported as exploratory in the Supplementary Information and does not replace the global-peak evaluation.

### 2.9 Reproducibility and provenance

Analysis rules were recorded in versioned human- and machine-readable files at the stage to which they applied:
- the Erken reconstruction contract;
- the supplementary event and double-logistic sensitivity protocols;
- the Erken observation-layer protocols;
- the Erken-only transfer freeze and its pre-performance amendment;
- the Vombsjön transfer execution specification.

Later changes were made only through new versions, with predecessors preserved.

The TIMESAT 4.4.1 and timesat-cli 1.9.2 runtimes were pinned to recorded source revisions, and their effective double-logistic defaults were captured in a frozen configuration snapshot. Execution was conditional on the runtime matching that snapshot and passing synthetic checks, including positive-affine equivariance of both TIMESAT reconstructions.

Machine-readable manifests record code revisions and SHA256 checksums of inputs, configurations, the execution specification and outputs. The Vombsjön transfer manifest records native MCI units, the processor roles, the absence of parameter tuning and of field data in tuning, and completion of a separate validation of the saved outputs. That validation covered scenario coverage, training-only scaling, paired masks, metric and aggregation calculations, peak denominators and field-comparison support.

All Vombsjön scenario–method fits completed. Computational completion was recorded separately from scientific reliability, and successful curve generation was not interpreted as evidence that a reconstructed property was preserved.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Observation support and lake-specific proxy evidence

The two-lake experiments differed in both observational support and evaluation target (Figure 1; Table 1). Erken provided a dense chlorophyll-fluorescence reference (CHLF) for evaluating temporal reconstruction under actual Sentinel-2 sampling, whereas Vombsjön provided observed satellite MCI for a locked transfer test. The satellite–chlorophyll comparisons supplied separate, lake-specific observed-proxy evidence. These evidence streams therefore addressed different aspects of reconstruction and proxy interpretation.

Across 2019–2025, Erken had 307 Sentinel-2-usable dates, of which 288 supplied sparse reconstruction inputs after intersection with open-water conditions and available reference measurements. These inputs covered seven years and retained CHLF as both the reconstructed quantity and the evaluation reference. The actual observation mask and secondary controlled-missingness experiments consequently evaluated temporal reconstruction of the same variable. Reconstruction common support was boundary-truncated in 2019 and 2025, limiting interpretation of their evaluated maxima to the available support.

The separate Erken observation-layer comparison contained 220 exact-date MCI–CHLF pairs and 215 NDCI–CHLF pairs on processor-shared common-support calendars. These counts describe satellite-index matchups and are distinct from the 288 reconstruction inputs. ACOLITE MCI was positively associated with CHLF, with Spearman ρ = 0.458 and a 95% year-cluster bootstrap interval of 0.295–0.610. This association provided lake-specific evidence connecting the observed MCI proxy to chlorophyll variability; it did not establish an absolute chlorophyll calibration or spatial and depth equivalence between satellite and in situ observations.

For Vombsjön, the independent processor calendars contained 335 ACOLITE, 419 L2A and 408 L1C observed dates during 2017–2026. ACOLITE was the frozen primary transfer processor, L2A a separate sensitivity and L1C a diagnostic baseline. Their fixed nominal-station 3×3 MCI series supported 1,200, 1,536 and 1,492 withholding scenarios, respectively (Table 1). These calendars were independent and were neither pooled nor used to rank processors. ACOLITE support in 2026 extended only through 3 August. The primary quantitative transfer target was withheld observed ACOLITE MCI.

Complementary Vombsjön field evidence comprised 54 field dates in 2018–2020, with seven valid fixed-polygon exact-date ACOLITE pairs and nine each for L2A and L1C. The ACOLITE pairs comprised four in 2019 and three in 2020; no processor had a valid 2018 pair. Paired ACOLITE field concentrations spanned approximately 1.206–33.9 µg/L, whereas the complete field dataset extended to approximately 125.7 µg/L. Thus, the paired evidence did not cover the 2018 extreme regime. Pair-level evidence is reserved for Supplementary Table S5; field Chl-a supplies complementary proxy-consistency context, not daily reconstruction truth.

Erken therefore provides the dense-reference reconstruction experiment, while Vombsjön provides a locked withheld-satellite transfer test with complementary but sparse field context.

### 3.2 Metric-specific reconstruction under the Erken actual observation mask

Reconstruction of the dense Erken reference under the actual observation mask showed different performance patterns for pointwise accuracy, global-peak timing and common-support seasonal integral (Figure 2). All three methods produced reconstructions with available metrics in each of the seven years, 2019–2025. Nevertheless, complete reconstruction output did not imply consistent preservation of the evaluated seasonal properties. Annual estimates and equal-year summaries revealed both differences among methods and substantial variation in peak-timing performance.

Linear interpolation had the lowest descriptive equal-year nRMSE, at 0.203, followed by TIMESAT smoothing spline at 0.223 and default TIMESAT double logistic at 0.250 (Figure 2a). The corresponding 95% whole-year cluster-bootstrap intervals were 0.154–0.250, 0.186–0.257 and 0.216–0.286. The annual results supported a consistent pointwise pattern: linear interpolation had lower nRMSE than default double logistic in all seven years and lower nRMSE than the smoothing spline in six of seven years. The spline had slightly lower nRMSE than linear interpolation in 2019. Linear interpolation therefore had the lowest equal-year nRMSE among the three evaluated methods under the actual observation mask.

Global-peak timing was substantially more year-sensitive than the pointwise nRMSE pattern (Figure 2b). Linear interpolation placed the reconstructed common-support maximum within ±10 days of the reference maximum in five of seven years; default double logistic and the smoothing spline each met this criterion in four years. Large timing errors were concentrated in 2020 and 2025. In 2020, absolute errors were 31 days for linear interpolation and 151 days for both default double logistic and the smoothing spline. In 2025, the corresponding errors were 200, 207 and 202 days. These annual extremes contributed strongly to equal-year mean absolute peak errors of 34.6, 54.4 and 54.6 days, respectively. The lower equal-year mean for linear interpolation therefore coexisted with substantial timing errors and did not establish stable timing superiority. These peak metrics refer to the global maximum within each year’s common support; because 2019 and 2025 were boundary-truncated, their common-support maxima are not interpreted as full-calendar-year ecological maxima.

The method pattern differed for the common-support seasonal integral (Figure 2c). Default double logistic had the lowest descriptive equal-year mean absolute integral error, at 126.0 µg·d/L, compared with 157.5 µg·d/L for linear interpolation and 187.1 µg·d/L for the smoothing spline. Paired whole-year bootstrap intervals for the double-logistic integral advantage included zero, so this lower mean does not establish statistical superiority. The contrast with timing was evident in 2020: default double logistic had an absolute integral error of only 6.2 µg·d/L despite its 151-day global-peak error. Thus, close agreement in the common-support integral could coexist with a large displacement of the reconstructed maximum.

Equal-year normalized absolute peak-magnitude error was 0.797 for linear interpolation, 1.039 for TIMESAT smoothing spline and 1.304 for default TIMESAT double logistic, using the common-support reference Q95−Q05 normalization.

Equal-year daily trajectory correlation was 0.864 for linear interpolation, 0.830 for TIMESAT smoothing spline and 0.742 for default TIMESAT double logistic (95% whole-year cluster-bootstrap intervals 0.817–0.911, 0.800–0.863 and 0.693–0.795, respectively).

Across the evaluated properties, linear interpolation combined lower pointwise error, lower equal-year normalized peak-magnitude error and higher daily trajectory correlation, whereas default double logistic had lower descriptive integral error. Global-peak timing remained strongly dependent on the year. Pointwise accuracy, seasonal timing, peak magnitude and integral preservation therefore provided distinct assessments of reconstruction reliability under the actual observation mask.

### 3.3 Reliability under controlled missingness in Erken

The actual observation mask established differences among reconstruction properties under the observed Sentinel-2 sampling pattern. Controlled additional missingness extended this comparison by examining how reliability varied with deletion-window duration, hidden-gap activity and reference global-peak containment (Figure 3). These experiments removed additional observations from the actual observation mask while retaining the same common-support boundaries.

Pointwise reconstruction error increased across the evaluated controlled consecutive deletion-window durations (Figure 3a). Between 10-day and 45-day windows, equal-year nRMSE increased from 0.208 to 0.238 for linear interpolation, from 0.253 to 0.289 for default TIMESAT double logistic and from 0.227 to 0.280 for TIMESAT smoothing spline. Linear interpolation retained the lowest equal-year nRMSE at each evaluated duration. These Erken windows represent calendar-day deletion intervals, distinct from the blocks of observed acquisitions used in the Vombsjön transfer experiment.

At a fixed 45-day duration, higher hidden-gap activity was associated with larger pointwise reconstruction error (Figure 3b). Across low, medium and high activity classes, equal-year nRMSE was 0.216, 0.237 and 0.273 for linear interpolation; 0.246, 0.282 and 0.316 for TIMESAT smoothing spline; and 0.274, 0.282 and 0.317 for default TIMESAT double logistic. Thus, error differed among activity classes even when the controlled deletion-window duration was held constant. The stratified pattern was consistent with positive equal-year summaries of within-year Spearman associations between continuous A_gap and nRMSE for all three methods at 45 days. A_gap was calculated retrospectively from the complete hidden reference, with activity classes defined by duration-specific tertiles; it is not available inside an unknown operational gap.

Peak timing success within ±10 days was lower when the controlled consecutive deletion window contained the reference global peak (Figure 3c). For 45-day windows with the peak outside the window, equal-year success proportions were 0.704 for linear interpolation, 0.517 for default TIMESAT double logistic and 0.563 for TIMESAT smoothing spline. With the peak inside the window, these proportions were 0.252, 0.387 and 0.445, respectively. For linear interpolation, the corresponding 95% whole-year cluster-bootstrap intervals were 0.414–0.990 with the peak outside and 0.067–0.524 with the peak inside. These broad intervals retain substantial annual uncertainty around the descriptive proportions. The timing pattern differed from the pointwise pattern: linear interpolation had the lowest nRMSE across the displayed duration and activity groups, but lower peak timing success than either TIMESAT method in the peak-containing stratum.

The secondary random-deletion experiment provided complementary evidence: stronger deletion was accompanied by increasing pointwise error, whereas peak-timing success did not decrease monotonically at every deletion level for all methods.

The summaries gave equal weight to the seven Erken years after within-year aggregation, with uncertainty represented by whole-year cluster-bootstrap intervals. Overlapping controlled-gap scenarios remained nested within years and did not constitute independent seasonal replicates.

The controlled experiments therefore identified reliability conditions associated with both the extent of additional missingness and the part of the seasonal signal covered by the deletion window. Longer windows and higher hidden-gap activity were associated with larger pointwise errors, while peak containment was associated with lower timing success and changed the descriptive method ordering. These results characterize metric-specific reliability within the evaluated Erken conditions without establishing a universal operational failure threshold.

### 3.4 Locked reconstruction of withheld Vombsjön ACOLITE observations

The Erken experiments established metric-specific reconstruction behavior under actual sampling and controlled additional missingness. The locked Vombsjön transfer then evaluated the three methods against withheld observed ACOLITE MCI (Figure 4). Reconstruction settings were frozen from Erken evidence before Vombsjön transfer performance was inspected and were applied without retuning. The primary target was observed MCI derived from ACOLITE rhos, rather than field Chl-a or daily ecological truth.

The primary evaluation included ten years, 2017–2026, with partial observed support through 3 August 2026. Available pointwise scenario counts per method were 315 for an isolated withheld acquisition and 305, 295 and 285 for blocks of two, three and four consecutive observed acquisitions, respectively. These blocks represent observed acquisitions, not fixed calendar-day gaps. All primary-method fits succeeded, so the displayed pointwise differences were not driven by differential method failure. Summaries gave equal weight to annual estimates; overlapping scenarios were not independent seasonal replicates.

Pointwise reconstruction error increased as more consecutive observed acquisitions were withheld (Figure 4a). Across isolated, two-, three- and four-acquisition withholding, equal-year nRMSE was 0.165, 0.196, 0.221 and 0.254 for linear interpolation; 0.170, 0.207, 0.238 and 0.271 for default TIMESAT double logistic; and 0.182, 0.232, 0.269 and 0.333 for TIMESAT smoothing spline. Linear interpolation had the lowest aggregate nRMSE in each design. Its 95% whole-year bootstrap interval was 0.136–0.194 under isolated withholding and 0.216–0.293 under four-acquisition withholding. These estimates use scenario-specific retained-training MCI Q95−Q05 normalization and are not numerically interchangeable with the dense-reference CHLF normalization used in Erken.

Withheld-date trajectory correlation generally weakened with more extensive withholding, although the intermediate responses were not uniformly monotonic (Figure 4b). Repeated predictions were median-collapsed within each method, year, withholding design, block size and date before annual correlations were calculated and summarized with equal year weight. Under isolated withholding, equal-year correlations were 0.755 for linear interpolation, 0.745 for default TIMESAT double logistic and 0.729 for TIMESAT smoothing spline. Under four-acquisition withholding, the corresponding values were 0.714, 0.663 and 0.694. Under four-acquisition withholding, the smoothing spline had higher trajectory correlation than default TIMESAT double logistic despite its higher nRMSE. These correlations describe agreement at withheld observation dates, rather than the dense daily trajectory agreement evaluated in Erken.

Conditional observed-proxy peak timing provided a separate secondary assessment (Figure 4c). The reference was the available, quality-controlled observed-MCI maximum within annual support, not the true ecological bloom peak. Only scenarios withholding an eligible reference peak entered this assessment. Consequently, its denominators were substantially smaller than those for pointwise evaluation: 10, 20, 30 and 40 reference-eligible scenarios per method across isolated, two-, three- and four-acquisition withholding, respectively.

Equal-year peak timing success within ±10 days was 0.40 for linear interpolation, 0.40 for default TIMESAT double logistic and 0.30 for TIMESAT smoothing spline under isolated withholding. Under four-acquisition withholding, the corresponding proportions were 0.275, 0.325 and 0.250. Default double logistic also had higher descriptive success proportions than the other methods under two- and three-acquisition withholding, despite its higher aggregate pointwise error than linear interpolation. Timing uncertainty remained broad: the four-acquisition success proportion for default double logistic had a 95% whole-year bootstrap interval of 0.075–0.575.

The locked transfer therefore retained a metric-specific reliability pattern. Withholding more consecutive observed acquisitions was accompanied by greater pointwise error, while trajectory agreement and conditional peak timing distinguished the methods differently. Linear interpolation’s lower aggregate nRMSE across all four primary ACOLITE designs did not imply uniformly higher reliability across the other evaluated properties.

### 3.5 Annual heterogeneity of the locked Vombsjön transfer

The aggregate transfer summaries concealed substantial annual variation in reconstruction performance. Figure 5 resolves this variation for primary ACOLITE MCI under four-acquisition withholding, the largest withholding block evaluated. Whereas Figure 4 summarizes equal-year behavior across withholding designs, Figure 5 shows how pointwise error, withheld-date trajectory correlation and conditional observed-proxy peak timing differed among the ten years from 2017 to 2026.

The annual pointwise results showed both consistent and variable method patterns (Figure 5a). Within the primary ACOLITE evaluation, linear interpolation had lower annual nRMSE than TIMESAT smoothing spline in every Vombsjön year and withholding design. Its comparison with default TIMESAT double logistic was less uniform: under isolated withholding, each method had lower annual nRMSE in five years, whereas linear interpolation had lower annual nRMSE in eight of ten years under four-acquisition withholding. The two exceptions under four-acquisition withholding were 2018 and 2020. In 2020, default TIMESAT double logistic had annual nRMSE of 0.225, compared with 0.276 for linear interpolation, reversing their aggregate ordering.

Annual withheld-date trajectory correlation also varied across years and did not reproduce the pointwise ordering consistently (Figure 5b). In 2020, correlation was 0.735 for default TIMESAT double logistic and 0.691 for linear interpolation, accompanying the lower double-logistic nRMSE in that year. In 2026, however, TIMESAT smoothing spline had the highest trajectory correlation among the three methods despite having higher nRMSE than linear interpolation. Thus, the annual comparison depended on whether agreement was evaluated through normalized pointwise error or correspondence among withheld-date values.

Conditional observed-proxy peak timing exposed further differences among years and methods (Figure 5c). Each annual mean absolute peak error summarized four reference-eligible peak scenarios per method-year. These scenarios were nested within the same year and did not represent four independent seasons. The reference remained the available quality-controlled observed-MCI maximum within annual support, rather than the true ecological bloom peak. In 2020, default TIMESAT double logistic had a mean absolute peak error of 5.25 days, compared with 42 days for linear interpolation. The same year therefore combined lower double-logistic nRMSE with substantially smaller conditional peak error, demonstrating that the annual departure from the aggregate pointwise ordering extended to another reconstruction property.

The partial 2026 support remained included in the annual and equal-year summaries. ACOLITE observed support extended only through 3 August 2026. Under four-acquisition withholding, annual nRMSE was 0.367 for linear interpolation, 0.433 for default TIMESAT double logistic and 0.421 for TIMESAT smoothing spline, while mean absolute observed-proxy peak errors were approximately 95–107 days across the three methods. These large timing errors describe reconstruction relative to the maximum within the available observations; that maximum is not interpreted as a full-calendar-year ecological maximum.

The 2018 results were also retained as part of the withheld-satellite-proxy evaluation. No valid contemporaneous fixed-polygon field pairs were available in 2018, so its transfer results do not constitute contemporaneous field validation.

Annual variation therefore materially qualified the aggregate transfer pattern. Lower equal-year pointwise error did not imply the same method ordering in every year, and annual nRMSE, trajectory agreement and conditional peak timing remained distinct assessments of reliability. Retaining all eligible years, including the difficult and partial-support cases, made these differences visible.

### 3.6 Sensitivity analyses and diagnostic evidence

The sensitivity and diagnostic analyses altered the relative ordering of reconstruction methods for individual metrics while retaining a metric-specific interpretation of reconstruction reliability. ACOLITE remained the primary Vombsjön processor under the frozen design, with L2A evaluated separately as a sensitivity and L1C as a diagnostic baseline. Their independent calendars and scenario counts (Table 1) precluded pooling or interpreting differences as processor superiority.

Pointwise nRMSE was higher under four-acquisition than isolated withholding for all three methods within both L2A and L1C. For L2A, equal-year nRMSE increased from 0.177, 0.174 and 0.193 under isolated withholding to 0.237, 0.245 and 0.307 under four-acquisition withholding for linear interpolation, default TIMESAT double logistic and TIMESAT smoothing spline, respectively. The corresponding L1C values increased from 0.204, 0.194 and 0.226 to 0.262, 0.276 and 0.329. Default double logistic had slightly lower isolated nRMSE than linear interpolation in both layers, whereas linear interpolation had lower nRMSE under four-acquisition withholding. Within L1C, the smoothing spline had the highest descriptive trajectory correlation and ±10-day peak-success rate under four-acquisition withholding despite its largest pointwise error.

L2A peak summaries had only nine available years. Its 2025 observed maximum occurred on 26 December, at the support boundary, and was non-identifiable for peak evaluation under the frozen rule. Reference-eligible peak-scenario counts were therefore 9, 18, 26 and 34 for isolated, two-, three- and four-acquisition withholding, respectively. The unavailable 2025 timing result remained distinct from its available pointwise evaluation.

The CV-selected double-logistic sensitivity altered individual metrics in different directions rather than producing a consistent improvement. Its separately frozen transfer setting was p_seapar = 0, while default TIMESAT double logistic retained p_seapar = 1 as the primary benchmark. For ACOLITE, isolated equal-year nRMSE changed only slightly, from 0.1701 to 0.1694, whereas four-acquisition nRMSE increased from 0.2711 to 0.2820. Isolated ±10-day peak success increased from 0.40 to 0.50, but equal-year mean absolute observed-proxy peak error increased from 22.3 to 39.0 days. The Erken actual-mask sensitivity likewise reduced equal-year nRMSE from 0.250 to 0.239 while increasing mean absolute common-support integral error from 126.0 to 139.5 µg·d/L. These within-lake trade-offs did not support replacing the default benchmark; numerical nRMSE levels remain non-comparable between lakes because their targets and normalization differ.

Peak-tolerance sensitivity also changed descriptive timing contrasts. Under ACOLITE four-acquisition withholding, all three primary methods had success proportions of 0.125 at ±5 days, whereas default double logistic had the highest proportion at ±10 and ±15 days. Changing tolerance therefore did not produce a uniform alternative method ordering. These estimates remained conditional on the available observed-MCI maximum, with only 10–40 reference-eligible scenarios across designs, nested within years. Neither narrower nor broader tolerances established true ecological bloom-peak validation or a biologically correct threshold.

Detailed missingness and sensitivity results are reserved for Supplementary Information. The supplementary event analysis remains secondary/exploratory because its protocol followed inspection of the global-maximum results; it does not replace the predefined global-peak evaluation. All 16,912 Vombsjön scenario–method fits, including the separate CV-selected sensitivity, completed successfully, but completion did not establish scientific reliability.

Together, these analyses supported the same metric-specific interpretation without preserving every numerical ranking. Reconstruction performance depended on the metric, withholding pattern, year and evidence layer, and the secondary analyses did not establish a uniform alternative to the predefined primary comparisons.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Reconstruction reliability is metric-specific

The two-lake comparison shows that temporal reconstruction reliability cannot be represented adequately by a single measure of fit. Linear interpolation provided a strong pointwise baseline, but its lower equal-year nRMSE did not imply consistent superiority in peak timing, trajectory agreement or seasonal integral preservation. These properties answer different observational questions: pointwise error describes discrepancies at evaluated dates, correlation describes correspondence in variation, and peak and integral metrics describe particular features of the reconstructed record. The disagreements among them are therefore central to interpreting reconstruction performance, rather than secondary exceptions to an overall method ranking.

The Erken experiment makes this distinction particularly clear because reconstruction and evaluation used the same CHLF variable. Default TIMESAT double logistic had the lowest descriptive equal-year absolute common-support integral error despite higher pointwise error than linear interpolation. In 2020, its integral error was only 6.2 µg·d/L while its global-peak timing error was 151 days. This combination demonstrates that agreement in accumulated signal does not establish preservation of the timing of the dominant maximum. Similarly, lower normalized peak-magnitude error does not ensure that the reconstructed maximum represents the correct date. The integral comparison remains descriptive; the paired uncertainty did not establish statistical superiority.

Vombsjön extended this separation of properties to withheld satellite observations. Under four-acquisition withholding, the smoothing spline had higher aggregate withheld-date trajectory correlation than default double logistic despite higher nRMSE, while default double logistic had higher conditional peak-timing success than linear interpolation. Even two timing summaries could differ in their method ordering, as shown by the CV-selected double-logistic sensitivity: a higher success proportion within a tolerance coexisted with larger mean absolute peak error. A tolerance-based success rate and a continuous error measure therefore provide complementary information about the same reconstruction task.

This interpretation builds on the use of fitted satellite trajectories to extract seasonal properties in TIMESAT (Jönsson and Eklundh, 2004). In Lake Balaton, Palmer et al. (2015b) found that smoothing choices differed in their representation of bloom events and selected Savitzky–Golay filtering for their application. The present results support evaluating the preserved properties explicitly, without transferring that study’s selected method to a different setting. They also qualify the apparent simplicity of linear interpolation’s strong baseline performance: a locally interpolated curve can perform well at evaluated dates while remaining insufficiently constrained for timing or cumulative quantities. No single observed advantage establishes general superiority of interpolation or seasonal fitting.

### 4.2 Missingness structure conditions reconstruction reliability

The controlled experiments show that reconstruction difficulty depends on which observations are missing as well as how many are available. In Erken, longer calendar-day deletion windows were associated with increasing pointwise error. At a fixed window duration, errors also differed with hidden-gap activity and peak containment. Observation density alone therefore cannot describe the conditions under which the methods were evaluated: it does not specify whether missing observations cover relatively stable reference values, rapid variation or the reference maximum. These distinctions help explain why an aggregate error score gives an incomplete account of seasonal reliability.

The activity measure A_gap provides evidence about this limitation, but only retrospectively. It was calculated from the complete Erken reference inside the deliberately hidden window. Its association with error shows that reconstruction performance differed according to the variation concealed by missingness. It does not supply an operational predictor for a real gap whose internal trajectory is unknown. The observed activity strata therefore describe reliability conditions in this experiment; they cannot be used directly to assign confidence to an unobserved Vombsjön interval or to prescribe an activity-dependent reconstruction rule.

Peak-containing windows further demonstrate the need to distinguish evaluation targets. Under the evaluated Erken conditions, linear interpolation’s lower pointwise error did not translate into the highest timing success when the reference peak was hidden. This finding concerns the common-support global maximum, whose date is a different feature from average agreement across the series. The supplementary event analysis asks a related but distinct question about recovery of multiple reference events. Because that analysis followed inspection of the global-maximum results, its secondary/exploratory status remains essential; it cannot replace an unfavorable primary timing result with a more favorable event-recovery summary.

Vombsjön showed higher pointwise error as more consecutive observed acquisitions were withheld, but acquisition blocks are not calendar-day windows. Their temporal spans depend on the available observation calendar, so a four-acquisition block cannot be assigned a common duration across years or processors. This distinction matters in optical records where cloud screening and invalid observations interrupt sampling; Palmer et al. (2015b) likewise identified missing observations as a limitation for lake phenology extraction. Together, the experiments support describing both temporal support and the missingness design. They do not establish a universal gap-duration threshold or imply that timing success declines monotonically with every increase in missingness.

### 4.3 What transferred from Erken to Vombsjön

Freezing reconstruction choices from Erken before examining Vombsjön performance made the second lake a test of previously specified choices. Reconstruction settings were not adjusted to improve the transfer results, and field observations did not enter tuning. This separation strengthens the interpretation of both successful and unsuccessful transfer behavior: the Vombsjön results describe how the retained methods performed under a new observational target and calendar. They do not show what could be achieved by selecting parameters retrospectively for that lake. ACOLITE’s primary status likewise followed the study design rather than a comparison showing it to be the best processor.

Several patterns carried across the two experiments. Stronger withholding was accompanied by greater pointwise error, linear interpolation remained an informative baseline, and agreement among reconstruction metrics was incomplete. A universal method ordering did not transfer. Default double logistic could perform better than linear interpolation in particular Vombsjön years and for conditional peak timing, while smoothing-spline behavior differed between pointwise error and correlation. The processor-specific sensitivities and the CV-selected double-logistic sensitivity also changed individual contrasts without providing a consistently better alternative. Transfer thus concerned the persistence of reliability trade-offs more than preservation of a fixed hierarchy of methods.

Annual results were necessary to identify those limits. In 2020, default double logistic combined lower nRMSE and higher withheld-date correlation than linear interpolation with substantially smaller observed-proxy peak error under four-acquisition withholding. The partial 2026 record instead showed large timing errors across methods and a different correlation ordering. These examples establish that the aggregate result does not describe every year; they do not identify the environmental causes of the differences. Retaining difficult and partial-support years makes the evaluated conditions visible. Robustness to removing individual Vombsjön years was not evaluated and cannot be inferred from retaining them.

The cross-lake comparison is consequently qualitative. Erken reconstructs CHLF and evaluates withheld values from a dense reference, whereas Vombsjön reconstructs satellite MCI and evaluates withheld observed acquisitions. Erken nRMSE uses the common-support reference Q95−Q05 scale; Vombsjön uses scenario-specific retained-training MCI Q95−Q05. Erken trajectory correlation describes daily common-support agreement, while Vombsjön correlation uses repeated withheld-date predictions collapsed within dates before annual evaluation. Calendar-day deletion windows and observed-acquisition blocks also impose different missingness. These differences prevent numerical skill levels from identifying which lake is easier to reconstruct. What transfers is evidence that method performance depends on the quantity evaluated and the support available.

### 4.4 Satellite proxy interpretation and limits of field validation

The observational meaning of the reconstructed variable limits what can be inferred from successful transfer. Red-edge indices have an established role in inland-water chlorophyll monitoring, and Salls et al. (2024) evaluated Sentinel-2 MCI through explicit calibration and validation against field concentrations. That distinction is important here: MCI remains a satellite proxy, not an absolute Chl-a concentration. As emphasized more generally for optically complex inland waters by Palmer et al. (2015a), retrieving water properties requires attention to the observational and optical context. Temporal agreement with a satellite index does not independently establish the accuracy of its relationship to chlorophyll.

The Erken MCI–CHLF association provides separate, lake-specific evidence that observed MCI contains information about chlorophyll variability. It complements the temporal masking experiment, which evaluates reconstruction of CHLF itself and therefore does not test satellite retrieval accuracy. The association neither defines a universal conversion from MCI to Chl-a nor demonstrates identical horizontal and vertical sampling. Maintaining these two Erken evidence streams separately allows the study to address both temporal information loss and proxy relevance without attributing satellite-retrieval validation to a same-variable reconstruction experiment.

The Vombsjön field observations provide more limited support. Only seven ACOLITE fixed-polygon exact-date pairs were valid, and these were confined to 2019–2020. No valid contemporaneous fixed-polygon field pair existed in 2018 for any processor, so the paired evidence did not sample the extreme field Chl-a regime recorded that year. The field record supplies ecological context and complementary observed-proxy consistency evidence, but it cannot validate the 2018 transfer result. Moreover, the fixed pelagic polygon used for field comparisons differs from the nominal-station 3×3 support of the temporal MCI series. Integrated-water-column samples are not assumed equivalent to satellite-surface Chl-a, and exact-date pairing does not remove those representativeness limits.

Primary Vombsjön evaluation therefore establishes agreement with withheld observed ACOLITE MCI under the specified designs. The reference for conditional peak timing is the maximum among available quality-controlled MCI observations within annual support. A maximum occurring between acquisitions or outside that support is not independently observed by this comparison. Changing the timing tolerance changes the criterion for agreement with the observed maximum, not its ecological status. This is why a reconstruction can reproduce withheld satellite values without proving recovery of daily ecological conditions or the true bloom peak. Numerical completeness of the reconstructed curve cannot close that evidential gap.

### 4.5 Implications, limitations and outlook

The practical implication is to align reconstruction evaluation with the intended downstream quantity. If the application concerns values at missing observation dates, pointwise error is directly relevant. If it concerns peak timing, peak magnitude or seasonal accumulation, those quantities require their own evaluation and adequate reference support. The present results support retaining simple interpolation as an explicit benchmark when evaluating comparable satellite time-series reconstruction problems, while testing whether more structured fits improve the property of interest. Reporting several properties is more informative than selecting a method from one aggregate error statistic, but the properties should remain separate rather than being combined into an unvalidated overall reliability score.

The scope for generalization remains limited to one dense-reference lake and one transfer lake. Seven Erken years and ten Vombsjön years provide the meaningful seasonal replication units; thousands of overlapping deletion or withholding scenarios do not constitute additional independent seasons. Whole-year summaries and uncertainty intervals preserve that distinction but cannot represent all lake types or sampling regimes. Erken’s 2019 and 2025 evaluations are restricted to boundary-truncated common support. ACOLITE support in Vombsjön ends on 3 August 2026, and L2A peak evaluation has an unavailable year. These qualifications are part of the evidence, not reasons to silently omit difficult outcomes.

The transfer also leaves several properties unvalidated. Sparse field matchups do not provide dense daily ecological truth, and the study cannot assess Vombsjön seasonal-integral accuracy, true unobserved bloom timing or onset and end of season. Independent ACOLITE, L2A and L1C calendars prevent a matched comparison of processors; their sensitivity and diagnostic results combine distinct observation support with distinct products. Nor do the reconstruction comparisons isolate causes of annual differences. The conclusions therefore concern the evaluated observational records and reconstruction tasks rather than general ecological predictability or atmospheric-correction superiority.

Future studies could extend the transfer to additional lakes and pair satellite records with denser field or autonomous optical observations. Explicitly matched processor calendars would address a different comparison from the independent-calendar evaluation here, while uncertainty-aware reconstruction could be tested against application-specific targets such as event timing or seasonal exposure. The central requirement is to judge reconstruction methods against the ecological or observational property they are intended to preserve, rather than assuming that successful curve fitting or low pointwise error implies reliable phenology.
